Ports Cheat-Sheet (common & reliable):
======================================
	Ports can vary by distro/controller; these are the widely used defaults.

Control plane & node:
 . kube-apiserver: 6443/TCP
 . kubelet: 10250/TCP (API), 10255/TCP (read-only, often disabled)
 . kube-scheduler: 10259/TCP
 . kube-controller-manager: 10257/TCP
 . etcd: 2379–2380/TCP

Core services & ranges:
 . NodePort range: 30000–32767/TCP
 . CoreDNS: 53/TCP,UDP
 . Metrics-server: 4443/TCP (webhook/secure)
 . Ingress NGINX (inside cluster): often 80/443 on the controller Pod; externally you’ll use:
	> LoadBalancer Service: cloud LB public port(s) (commonly 80/443)
	> NodePort: chosen NodePorts (if not using LB)

1. What is a Kubernetes Service?

Imagine Swiggy or Zomato — there are thousands of restaurants (just like there can be hundreds of pods in Kubernetes), and each restaurant’s location (pod IP) changes often.
If you as a customer had to directly remember each restaurant’s changing address, you’d give up quickly.

This is where the Service comes in.
In Kubernetes:
 > Pods are temporary — they can die, restart, and get new IPs.
 > Services are permanent — they have a fixed “contact number” or “front desk” that always forwards your request to the right pod(s).

So, a Service is like Swiggy’s main system that knows:
 . Which restaurants (pods) are available
 . Where they are located right now
 . How to forward the customer’s order (request) to the right restaurant (pod)

2. Types of Kubernetes Services and How They Work in the Background
A. ClusterIP (Default)
📍 Analogy: This is like the internal restaurant kitchen network inside Swiggy — the chefs talk to delivery staff inside the same restaurant, but customers can’t directly access it.

Real background in Kubernetes:
 . Creates a virtual IP address (stable) that is only reachable inside the cluster.
 . Other pods inside the cluster can call this Service to communicate.
 . Example: Your backend API talking to the database — no public access needed.

Flow in real-time:
1. A pod wants to talk to another pod (like the backend API calling the DB).
2. Instead of searching for the DB pod’s current IP, it calls the ClusterIP.
3. Kubernetes Service forwards the request to one of the DB pods using kube-proxy.
4. All this happens only inside the cluster — no outside user can connect.

B. NodePort
📍 Analogy: This is like Swiggy giving a special phone number for customers to directly call a restaurant, but only through certain fixed entry points (nodes).

Real background in Kubernetes:
 . Exposes the Service on a specific port on every node in the cluster.
 . You can access it via <NodeIP>:<NodePort> from outside the cluster.
 . Usually for testing or small setups (not for large-scale public access).

Flow in real-time:
1. Kubernetes opens the same port (like 32000) on all worker nodes.
2. You connect to that port from outside, it forwards to the right pod.
3. It’s like calling any branch of Swiggy’s office and still getting connected to your restaurant.

C. LoadBalancer
📍 Analogy: This is Swiggy/Zomato’s public call center number — no matter which city you’re in, you dial the same number, and they automatically send your order to the nearest restaurant.

Real background in Kubernetes:
 . Integrates with cloud provider load balancers (AWS ELB, Azure LB, GCP LB).
 . Gives you a public IP so users on the internet can access your app.
 . Balances traffic across multiple pods and nodes.

Flow in real-time:
1. You request the application using the public IP/DNS given by the cloud LB.
2. The load balancer routes your request to a healthy node.
3. Kubernetes Service inside the node sends it to the right pod.
4. If one pod dies, requests are automatically sent to others.

D. Ingress (Special case, not exactly a Service but works with them)
📍 Analogy: Instead of just one public number, imagine Swiggy having one number for all restaurants, but you press 1 for pizza, 2 for biryani, 3 for desserts. The system routes you to the right place.

Real background in Kubernetes:
 . Works with an Ingress Controller (like Nginx, Traefik).
 . Allows HTTP/HTTPS routing based on domain name or path.
 . Lets multiple apps share the same public IP but be reached differently.
 . Example: pizza.swiggy.com → Pizza service; biryani.swiggy.com → Biryani service.

Flow in real-time:
1. The Ingress Controller receives the HTTP request.
2. Checks the domain/path.
3. Sends it to the correct Kubernetes Service, which then goes to the right pod.

Simple Story: How Your Click Reaches the Right Pod
==================================================

Think of it like sending a letter to the right house in a big city.

1. Browser asks for the address
 > You type a website name.
 > The internet’s “phonebook” (DNS) finds the exact street address (IP).

2. Traffic policeman at the city gate
 > The address points to a big gate (Load Balancer).
 > The gate decides which entrance road the request should take into the city.

3. Gate sends to the right neighborhood
 > An Ingress Controller or Service acts like a postal sorting office — it looks at the label (rules) and sends your letter to the correct neighborhood (application).

4. Neighborhood delivery truck
 > A helper called kube-proxy picks a house (Pod) in that neighborhood.
 > It keeps a list of all active houses and chooses one fairly.

5. Letter reaches the house
 > The road system (CNI network) makes sure your letter reaches the exact house’s front door (Pod IP).
 > The person inside (your app) opens the letter and replies.
